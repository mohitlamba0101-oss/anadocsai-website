Scenario 1 · Polaris migration decision

Should this FP&A model move to Polaris, and what would it cost?

The situation

A Classic planning model of 52 modules and about 700 line items had hit its workspace limit twice in a year. Polaris was on the roadmap, but every estimate of the migration effort was a guess, and the team feared the formulas that would stop working.

What AnaDocs showed

  • Engine detection confirmed Classic from the exports and sized the model against Anaplan’s published cell costs.
  • 12 line items will not work as exported (LOOKUPs on a coarser time scale and mapping dimensions Polaris rejects), 57 will calculate differently and need reconciling, and 184 patterns work but would hurt performance in Polaris.
  • A phased plan: 48 delivery days plus 14 client-side days, 11 weeks to cutover including a parallel run, priced at the team’s own day rate, with the assumptions editable on the page.
  • Fix Studio produced paste-ready rewrites for the blockers, and the remediation workbook listed every finding by module.

What changed

  • The migration was approved with a budget grounded in a count of real problems, not cell counts.
  • The 12 blockers were fixed in the Classic model first, so the move itself carried no surprises.
  • The plan and the workbook became the project charter.

Scenario 2 · The builder has left

A 234-module model nobody could explain any more

The situation

The architect who built a 234-module, 2,000-line-item commercial planning model over four years left the company. Three weeks later a quarter-end change was needed in a module whose formulas ran to 400 characters, and nobody dared touch it.

What AnaDocs showed

  • Every formula in plain English with its references, and a lineage view showing what feeds each line item and what depends on it, across modules.
  • Module purposes and a business glossary generated from the structure, so the new builder could read the model like a document.
  • The Copilot answered questions such as “which modules feed the margin reporting?” and cited the exact module and line item for every claim.
  • A handover pack and the full documentation site, exported for the team.

What changed

  • The new builder made the quarter-end change in two days instead of the fortnight the team had planned for.
  • The documentation is regenerated after every release, so the knowledge no longer lives in one head.
  • Onboarding for the next hire started from the handover pack rather than from screen-shares.

Scenario 3 · The model that got slow

Finding the 31% of memory nobody was using

The situation

A supply-planning model had grown to 110 GB. Saves took minutes, the nightly process overran, and the team was about to request a bigger workspace at a five-figure cost.

What AnaDocs showed

  • The Optimisation report for Classic listed where memory and calculation time went, ranked by impact, each rule traced to Anaplan’s own guidance and the Planual.
  • Summary methods left on by default on large line items, text line items dimensioned by day, and two staging modules nobody read any more accounted for 31% of the model.
  • The health score rose from 58 to 82 as the quick wins were applied; every change came with a paste-ready fix and a “how to verify” step.
  • The Model diff confirmed that no formula logic had changed, only size.

What changed

  • The workspace upgrade was cancelled; the model fell to 76 GB.
  • The nightly process finished inside its window again.
  • The report is re-run before each release to stop the creep returning.

Scenario 4 · Already on Polaris

Tuning a Polaris model when the grid says 361,000 TiB

The situation

An insurance model had been built on Polaris from day one. Classic-era tooling priced it as a dense grid of 73 quadrillion cells and recommended a migration it had already made. The real size was 17 GB, and nobody knew where it went.

What AnaDocs showed

  • AnaDocs read the reported size and the populated cell counts, detected Polaris, and showed the real 17 GB with the per-module split instead of an impossible dense figure.
  • The Polaris track of the Optimisation report ranked the tuning backlog in the order Anaplan’s own Polaris guidance recommends: “All cells” formulas first, then one-to-many LOOKUP chains, then summary methods on large line items.
  • No migration plan, cutover or effort estimate was shown, because the model already runs on Polaris; the page says so and points to the tuning list.

What changed

  • The team rewrote the eleven “All cells” formulas that drove most of the populated-cell growth.
  • Calculation effort on the heaviest module fell by half, measured in Anaplan after the change.
  • The tuning backlog became a standing item in the release checklist.

Scenario 5 · The Centre of Excellence

Eight models, one data hub, and three risks nobody had written down

The situation

A CoE ran eight models across three workspaces, fed by a data hub. Each model had an owner; nobody owned the connections between them. A list changed in one model broke a report in another every few months.

What AnaDocs showed

  • Build my estate pulled all eight models in one background job, and the estate map drew the real feeds from Anaplan’s import definitions: six confirmed imports, four file hand-offs and one CloudWorks flow.
  • The data hub was flagged as a hub and single point of failure for the UAT model.
  • Risks with evidence: the SKU list differed across four models, the test model was one revision behind development, two feeds had failed on their last run, and one import pointed at a model the login could no longer see.
  • The Consolidation tab found the same cost-allocation module rebuilt in three models.

What changed

  • List ownership moved to the hub, with drift now caught by the estate instead of by a broken report.
  • The two failed feeds were repaired the same day; the stale UAT copy was synced.
  • The quarterly CoE review now opens with the estate map and the risk list.
